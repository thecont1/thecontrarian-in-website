### Making of a political animal

I first met Suchitra in a tiffin room near her parents’ bungalow in a tony neighbourhood in central Chennai in 2009. Back then, she was an amateur photographer and I used to earn a living shooting weddings across India. She went on to hire me as photographer for her grand wedding. (Without getting into details, let me just say that it was a disastrous commission that ended in withheld payments.) I later learned that she moved to New York and set up her own wedding photography business. It was short-lived as she then went to study international relations at Yale University. 

In 2013, India-U.S. relations were considerably strained when one of our diplomats in the New York consulate was charged with human trafficking. The case was so high profile that it was eventually taken up by a U.S. District Attorney, a rule-of-law crusader better known for prosecuting the Five Families and al-Qaeda. We cried racism, as we often do when we are perceived to be the victims of it, and pulled no diplomatic punches to aggressively resolve it. Suchitra, in the early days of promoting herself as a political pundit, came to the defense of her &lsquo;friend&rsquo; and appealed to the influential Indian-American community against the &lsquo;stigma of social shaming&rsquo; and for the allegations &lsquo;not to be tried in the court of public opinion or by journalism of outrage.&rsquo; It sounds utterly hypocritical to read it now. 

<img src="/library/throwaways/suchitra-vijayan-making-of-the-political-animal/image53.png" alt="The heavily-publicised and free-to-participate UN/DO Photography Workshop raised sponsorship from the MurthyNAYAK Foundation, a Baltimore-Bangalore based NGO. A crass rendition of the already crass original." style="max-width: 450px; max-height: 700px; margin-top: 0em;">

Meanwhile, my career progressed into that of a documentary photographer and artist with a focus on contemporary Indian society and culture. My first book <em>Matrimania</em> (p. Hatje Cantz, 2019), a critical take on wedding culture in India, arrived at my doorstep in Bangalore from the printers in Italy just days after the polis project launched their smear campaign fuelled by a coterie of followers. In the low-volume, high-capital world of photobooks, promotion and sales activities in the first few months are critical. Regrettably, Matrimania was doomed to share the taint meant for <em>The African Portraits</em>. Its crowdfunding campaign had to be instantly aborted. The polis project roped in celebrities in the creative circuit (such as the 2020 Booker Prize nominee Maaza Mengiste) to comment on the article, which they then did publicly without knowing my work other than through its false representation.

### Wolf in critic's clothing

<img src="/library/throwaways/suchitra-vijayan-making-of-the-political-animal/image31.jpg" alt="&lsquo;Takeaways&rsquo; from the UN/DO Photography Workshop of October 2019 shared by one of the participants. No further details about what transpired during the event are available." style="max-width: 450px; max-height: 500px; margin-top: 0em;">

In 2017, an egregious case of plagiarism rocked the worldwide photo industry. With an impassioned criticism of the photo world (&ldquo;Datta sold snake oil to those who wanted snake oil&rdquo;), Suchitra emerged out of nowhere as the voice of our collective anger and disappointment. Around the same time, I was taking <em>The African Portraits</em> in a direction I wasn’t entirely sure of. In good faith, I reached out to Suchitra for a portfolio review so as to be able to see my work in new ways. We never did get around to having that discussion then, and now we find ourselves here. 

In <em>Why Do Photography Critics Hate Photography?’</em> (Boston Review, 2008), an essay I find myself returning to frequently, the art critic Susie Linfield aligns the haters with &ldquo;the forces of the most deplorable backwardness... with the frenzied crowds in Kabul and Karachi, Damascus and Tehran, who called for the execution of the Danish cartoonists... for, those demonstrators too, view images as an exploitation, an insult, a blasphemy, an act of subjugation.&rdquo; To call the polis project art critics would be an insult to art critics for the intellectual honesty that is expected of them. &ldquo;Criticism demands of the critic a terrible nakedness,&rdquo; wrote poet and critic Randall Jarrell in <em>The Age of Criticism</em> (1952): &ldquo;... and yet he must regard [the] self as no more than the instrument through which the art is seen, so that the work of art will seem everything to him and his own self nothing.&rdquo;

With a take-no-prisoners approach, Suchitra has weaponized critique to grab power in the online space. The polis project’s relationship with photography is defined more by contempt for the industry rather than any affection for the medium. They are not so much cultural theorists as they are visual moralists and ideological pessimists. And so they seek to ‘UN/DO Photography’ with clenched fist and sickle.

### Slipping down a slope, darkly

<p class="dropcap">It would be no exaggeration to say that 2019 was the darkest year in India’s modern history yet. Imagine a tragedy calendar so fully booked (Pulwama attack > Balakot strike > Modi II > Kashmir/370 > anti-CAA protests > the Delhi riots) that the global onset of COVID-19 came as relief. In the moditrumpistan in which we find ourselves, what we need is more credible, bias-adjusted journalism. What we get instead is a new breed of social media guerrillas who, while ruing mainstream media’s loss of moral compass, propose themselves as the all-encompassing solution. The polis project swiftly moved into that space, refashioning themselves as a ‘research and journalism organization’ and became shrill opinion shifters. They never amplified balanced, centrist voices except by way of ridicule. In a miserable year, Suchitra multiplied her personal cache of twitterati six-fold using the same divisive techniques as any far-right politician you can name, and then used this to raise funds for pet ventures like the UN/DO Photography Workshop. (Interestingly, throughout this period, the ‘racism and misogyny’ article remained a pinned post on Suchitra’s twitter. Talk about priorities.)</p>

I came across an illuminating article in POLITICO Magazine about the Southern Poverty Law Centre and its controversial founder Morris Dees. The SPLC is a non-profit dedicated to fighting racism, discrimination, hate, and most notably, the Ku Klux Klan. What could possibly go wrong? (Read: Has a Civil Rights Stalwart Lost Its Way?) As a thought experiment, if one treats the SPLC and Dees as analogous to the polis project and Suchitra, the parallels between the two organizations are curiously striking. The polis project (and the various postmodern neoliberal projects born in the decade following the 2008 financial crisis) might be heading down a dangerous path of activist journalism. One of the SPLC’s innovations, for instance, is the Hate Map that tracks hate groups across the U.S. A recent spate of misidentifications of people and groups prompted a closer look into its process and ideology. The polis project too tried their hand at hate maps and came up with one that visualises gang rapes across India… by size of gang! Ben Schreckinger of POLITICO follows the money and writes in his detailed investigation, “In America, even fighting racism can be very good business.” 

At the time of writing this, Suchitra is engaged in a public spat with the students of the National University of Juridical Sciences, Kolkata. Again, spinning a bundle of questionable claims, she is targeting an outspoken student for politely challenging her in a public forum and fanning her 50,000-strong twitter mob to pressure the university to have him removed. The young law students are having none of it: they are openly questioning the hubris of the good barrister and calling out her blatant abuse of power.

In an interview with the students of the same university many years ago, when they still looked up to her, Suchitra said she wanted to pass on the lesson she claimed to have learned from her criminal law professor: “...that you have to reserve the right to change your mind or reserve the right to change your ideas.” In light of all the evidence and arguments I’ve presented in my essay, maybe now is the time for Suchitra Vijayan to show some humility and take her professor’s advice both in letter and in spirit.

## End Notes: Fallout and Fall Guys

![2020 hindsight](/library/throwaways/suchitra-vijayan-making-of-the-political-animal/image5.png)

2020 brought clarity of vision to hindsight.

Instagram reminds me that nearly 104 weeks have passed since the anonymous accusations were floated on the anonymously edited @herdsceneand. This double-layered shield of anonymity—designed to thwart any sincere attempt at restorative justice for either side—is what a Delhi high court judge referred to as &ldquo;a ghost and a superghost&rdquo; while hearing a lawsuit filed by an artist who was called out on the same blog five days after I was. That verdict, delivered in favour of the artist, was yet another milestone in India's contentious track record with privacy and freedom of speech. (So much for cutting out BS and power play.)

In my own case, Accuser #1 turned out to be a misunderstanding at best and pettiness at worst. The Alkazi Foundation concluded as much after I shared chat transcripts following hours of interviews with their investigation committee. Later I found her getting invited to opportunities at the polis project: a podcast interview and to lecture at the UN/DO Photography Workshop. Accuser #2 played a true masterstroke. How does one prove a moment was false if that moment truly never occurred in the first place? Any attempt at trying to find verity in the said incident invariably led to dead ends.

With no possibility of resolution in sight, I was left to face never-seen-before consequences: 404 errors in place of long published work; emails that open with a gut-wrenching &lsquo;it has been brought to our attention that'; donations made in favour of pandemic relief efforts turned down as if it were ill-gotten wealth; feminist lawyers refusing pick up the phone; and finally, most dreaded of all, the joy of being published after a long dry spell diminished by the looming online presence of the polis project's hit piece.

The Alkazi Foundation, the most reputable face of Indian photography worldwide, in their wisdom, made no public statement about their findings, removed the jury's comments from the website, set an unachievable task for me to obtain my grant money, took the year off in the hope of a blow over, and came back in 2020 with a new grant cycle halved in value. Serious art institutions can and should do better.

Meanwhile, the very first Indian #metoo incident was proven to rest on a bed of lies. A desperate power grab prompting a call for an honest reckoning of its fallout and fall guys. Behind every avalanche is a snowflake that had to fall.

The writing of this rebuttal has forced me to introspect on my work in ways that I did not do before, and for that I am thankful. Suchitra Vijayan—a formidable opponent to rationalists and free thinkers anywhere—may even earn herself a footnote at the back of my book when I get around to publishing it. However, in the end I want to believe the conventional wisdom on the back of every rupee coin and note: that truth alone conquers.
